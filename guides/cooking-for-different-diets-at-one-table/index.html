How do you cook dinner when everyone at the table eats differently?

Build the meal from a shared base that fits the strictest eater, then let people add the extras that suit them: meat, cheese, a sauce, a topping. Serving the parts separately, in bowls or on a platter, lets a vegetarian, a vegan, a meat eater and someone avoiding lactose each assemble a plate from one pot of cooking instead of four.

Start from the strictest list

Write down who eats what. A typical mixed table:

  • One or more vegetarians (no meat or fish).
  • A vegan (no meat, fish, dairy, eggs or honey, depending on how they define it).
  • A pescatarian (fish and seafood, no other meat).
  • Someone avoiding lactose, which is different from a milk allergy.
  • Someone who simply will not eat mushrooms, or cilantro, or anything with a visible onion.

Plan the base around the most limited list. If the vegan can eat it, nobody else is left out of the base, and the others can add to it.

Cook the base, add on the side

This is the approach home cooks keep returning to, and it works for most weeknights:

  • Bowls and plates built at the table. Rice or noodles, roasted vegetables, beans or tofu, then sauces and toppings in small dishes. Chicken, sausage or cheese go in their own dish.
  • Tacos, fajitas, curries and soups. One pot of a vegetable or bean version, and a separate pan of meat for the people who want it. Cook them in that order, so the shared pot stays shared.
  • Pasta and a sauce on the side. Plain pasta and two sauces cover most tables.
  • Pizza and flatbreads. One crust, several toppings, one half with cheese and one without.

Keep hidden ingredients in mind. Stock, sauces and spice blends often contain animal products or dairy; a quick look at the label tells you which version you have.

Dislikes are not diets

A dislike is a preference and can be negotiated. A diet is a rule the person follows, and an allergy is a medical matter for a doctor. Write them on different lines. Mixing them up leads either to cooking around every dislike or to brushing off a real restriction as a preference.

Lactose and milk allergy are not the same

Avoiding lactose is a digestive matter; a milk allergy is an immune reaction. Some foods that are a problem for one person are not for the other, which is why they should be tracked separately. Ask the person, or their doctor, which applies.

Be honest about the limits

There will be nights a shared base does not exist. Plan one or two dinners a week where people eat different versions of a dish, and cook a freezer portion on easier nights so the hard nights have a backup. The goal is fewer dinners that need a second pan, not none.

FAQ

Can you cook one dinner for vegetarians and meat eaters?

Yes, in most cases. Cook a vegetarian base and add meat to a separate portion, or serve the meat as its own dish alongside. Bowls, tacos, curries and pasta with separate sauces all work this way.

What is the difference between avoiding lactose and a milk allergy?

Avoiding lactose is about a sugar in milk that some people digest poorly. A milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk proteins. They need different handling, so keep them as separate entries on your list and ask a doctor which applies.

How do I avoid cooking a separate meal for every person?

Choose a base dish that fits the strictest eater, and serve the extras on the side. Keep the number of fully separate dinners to one or two a week.

Can Pinjula plan for a vegan and a meat eater at the same table?

Yes. You add each person and their diet, and the suggestions fit the whole table. Vegetarian, vegan and pescatarian are decided from each recipe's ingredients.
