How do you meal plan when someone in the family has a food allergy?

Start with a written list of what each person at the table cannot eat, confirmed with their allergist, and plan shared dinners that work for everyone instead of cooking a second meal. Then read the full ingredient list on every packaged item each time you buy it, because labels and recipes change. An app can do the first pass of filtering recipes, but the last check is always yours.

Write down the list once

Meal planning gets easier when the rules live on paper (or in a note on your phone) instead of in your head. For each person, write:

  • The exact allergens, as your allergist or doctor described them. "Tree nuts" and "peanuts" are different entries; so are "milk" and "lactose intolerance".
  • Anything the person avoids by preference rather than allergy (mushrooms, olives). Keep these on a separate line so nobody confuses a dislike with an allergy.
  • Which products you have already checked and bought again, with the brand.

If you are not sure what to list, that is a question for the allergist, not for a recipe site or an app.

Plan around what everyone can eat

Dietitians and parent groups tend to give the same advice: start from the foods the allergic person can eat, and build the family menu from there, so one dinner serves the whole table. A child who sits at the same meal as everyone else is not singled out, and you cook once.

Practical ways to do this:

  • Pick the shared base first. Rice, pasta, potatoes, roasted vegetables, a pot of beans, a soup. Add the parts that only some people eat (a sauce, a topping, a cheese) on the side.
  • Keep a short list of dinners that already work. Five to eight is enough. Rotate them and add one new dish at a time after you have read its full ingredient list.
  • Plan only a few dinners ahead. Planning three or four dinners for the week, with the shopping list that goes with them, removes most of the daily "what's for dinner" question without locking you in.
  • Cook extra of a dinner everyone likes. A frozen portion is a backup for the night someone refuses the plan.

Read labels every time

A recipe can only tell you what its own ingredient list contains. It cannot know how a jar, a stock cube or a bag of flour was made, whether it shares a production line with an allergen, or whether the manufacturer changed the formula since last month. "May contain" wording on packages is a manufacturer's choice, not a guarantee in either direction. So:

  • Read the ingredient list on the package in your hand, every time, even for a product you have bought before.
  • Prepare the allergic person's food first, on clean surfaces and with clean utensils, if cross-contact matters for them. Ask the allergist how strict you need to be.
  • Tell other adults who cook for your child exactly what the list is.

Decide the house rule, with your doctor

Some families keep the allergen out of the house entirely; others keep it but separate. This depends on the allergen, the severity and the household, and it is a decision to make with the allergist. Nothing on this page replaces that conversation.

FAQ

Do I need to cook a separate meal for the person with the allergy?

Often not. Choose a base dish everyone can eat and add the parts only some people eat on the side. When a dish cannot be adapted, a separate portion made first, with clean tools, is the usual approach. Your allergist can tell you how careful you need to be.

How many dinners should I plan ahead?

Three or four is a workable start. It gives you a shopping list and removes the daily decision, without a full-week plan you will abandon on Wednesday.

Can an app tell me a recipe is okay for my child?

No. An app can remove recipes that contain the allergens you list, which saves time. It cannot read the label of the product you buy or know how it was made. Recipes containing the allergens you list are filtered out. Always check the full ingredient list yourself.
