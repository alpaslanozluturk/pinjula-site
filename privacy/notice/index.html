What stays on your phone

Most of what the app knows about you never leaves your phone. It is stored in the app's own storage, mostly its local database. We never receive the people you cook for, their allergies, diets or dislikes, your pantry or your ratings and notes. A few items below can travel when you use a feature, and they are marked:

  • The people you cook for, you included: the name typed for them, age band, portion size, whether they eat from a separate plate, an optional goal, and their allergies with severity, intolerances (lactose included), diets and disliked ingredients. Some of these people may be children. The app tells you once, the first time you enter it, that it stays on your phone. Only the number of people you cook for can leave, with a "Write me a recipe" request.
  • Your profile and settings (the app's language goes with AI requests and reports); your kitchen equipment and staples; your pantry, shopping list, calendar (a dish name you typed onto it goes with the AI features that use it) and week plans, and your cooking history; your favourites and Cookbook; your ratings with their notes. Ratings are not added up across users.
  • Your badges and a short log of the actions they count, by day and never by time, never with what you typed or shared.
  • A recipe the app wrote for a dish you typed, for seven days.
  • Your sign-in session, if you are signed in. Its access token is not private from us: it goes to our server when the app asks for a Premium ticket or when you delete your account, and to our sign-in service when the session renews.
  • Your reminder settings and what is prepared for reminders and widgets; your answers to the app's permission questions, with their dates; the dish pictures the app has downloaded, up to about 80 MB; a small record that keeps the app's occasional Premium suggestions rare; and, while usage statistics are on, the random ID the statistics library created.

Reminders and widgets are made on your phone from this data. Nothing is sent to make them, and there is no push service behind them. Anyone who can see your screen can see them, so they never name an allergy, a diet, an intolerance or the person who declared it.

Sharing. The share button hands a plain-text recipe or menu, or a photo of a dish you cooked, to your phone's share sheet. Where it goes is your choice; we are not involved and see nothing of it. The photo comes from your phone's own picker, is reduced in size, is never uploaded, and may carry whatever your phone stored in the file, such as when and where it was taken. The app never asks for your location.

Device backups. Your phone may include this app's data in its normal backup to your own Google or Apple account, and in a transfer to a new phone. Your phone makes that backup; we receive nothing from it and cannot read it. This means the backup may hold the allergies, diets and other health-related or belief-related information you entered, in your own account with Google or Apple. You can turn the backup off in your phone's settings.

You can change or delete most of this in the app. Deleting your account deletes the app's data from the phone you delete it on, with three exceptions: the small record that keeps the Premium suggestions rare is trimmed, not deleted; the membership library keeps its own on-device cache of your plan; and on iPhone, Google's own sign-in session in the keychain can outlast uninstalling the app. Uninstalling the app deletes what it keeps in its own storage.

The information we collect

Without an account. Nothing about you is sent to us, with two exceptions that need no account: the app downloads dish pictures from our server, and you can send a report about a recipe. If you allow usage statistics, those go to PostHog.

Technical data every connection shows. Every request to our server shows it your IP address and technical headers, such as the version of the software making it. Cloudflare, which runs our server, keeps a record of each request — the time, the address requested, the response status, your IP address, the request's technical headers and the approximate location Cloudflare works out from the IP address (country, region, city, postal code and coordinates) — for up to seven days, to deliver the request and to find faults and attacks. It does not include the body of a request. Our server's own log lines hold counts and error codes only, never an IP address, an account, a dish name you typed or anything the AI wrote. To stop abuse, our server also keeps short-lived counts of requests per address in memory; it does not store the address for this.

Dish pictures. The pictures of the catalogue's dishes are not inside the app. The app downloads the pictures of the dishes it suggests, and any picture it does not have when it shows a dish. The request names only the picture, which is the name of the catalogue dish, and carries no account and nothing else about you or your household. But the dishes it names are the ones the app suggested, which were chosen on your phone with your household's filters, so the pictures requested from one address can indirectly reflect what your household avoids — the same point as for the AI menu below. Cloudflare keeps the address and the picture's name for up to seven days, and we use them only for faults and attacks. Because each downloaded file is named after its dish, the pictures stored on your phone show which dishes the app has displayed.

Your account (only if you sign in). You sign in with Google or Apple. We have no password of our own and never see yours. From Google we receive your name, email address, the address of your profile picture, your Google account ID and, for a Google Workspace account, its domain. From Apple we receive your email address (a relay address if you chose "Hide My Email") and your Apple account ID. On iPhone the app asks Apple for your name too, but passes on only Apple's sign-in token, so we neither receive nor store that name. On Android, Apple signs you in through your browser and may share your name; if it does, our sign-in service stores it. Our sign-in service, run by Supabase in Frankfurt, keeps these, the times you signed in, and your sessions — with the IP address and the app's user agent — until you sign out (when your phone can reach the sign-in service), they expire, or you delete your account. It also logs each sign-in event with its IP address, account ID and email address. Our own account record is an internal number linked to your sign-in; it holds no email address and no name.

On your phone, the sign-in library keeps your session in the app's own storage. On iPhone, Google's own sign-in library also keeps its session for a Google account in the phone's keychain, which can outlast uninstalling the app; the app signs it out when you sign out and disconnects it when you delete your account. Signing out keeps the app's data on the phone. If a different account signs in on the same phone, the app warns you first and, to continue, deletes the previous account's data from the phone, so that two people's data never mix.

For each charge we also keep a minimal purchase record (see "Premium, payments and subscription emails").

AI requests, reports and usage statistics are described in their own sections below. Emails you send us: what you write, and the address you write from.

We do not collect precise location from your phone, government IDs, biometric data, payment card details (the store takes payment), your contacts or photo library, or advertising identifiers. The AI features and your permission

Premium has three features that use AI: a menu that mixes cuisines ("Mix them into one menu"), a plan for cooking a whole menu ("How to prepare this menu") and a recipe for a dish you typed ("Write me a recipe"). Each starts only when you use it, never in the background. Each sends a request to our server, which passes it to Google's Gemini API and returns the answer. Each needs an account and Premium.

What each one sends.

  • Mix them into one menu: the dishes your phone has already picked as candidates — their catalogue code, cuisine, name, role, courses and hands-on time — with the app's language, the cuisines you chose, how many menus you want, the courses you asked for and your time limit.
  • How to prepare this menu: each dish in the menu — its name, role, the names of its ingredients and its steps — and the app's language. For a dish you typed onto your calendar, that is the name you typed and the recipe the app wrote for it.
  • Write me a recipe: the name you typed, the app's language and how many people you are cooking for. Before offering this, the app checks on your phone that the name looks like a dish name; if it doesn't, nothing is sent.

Your allergies, diets and pantry, and who is in your household, are never sent (only how many people you are cooking for, with a typed dish). But the list of dishes sent for a menu was chosen on your phone with your household's filters, so it can indirectly show what your household avoids — for example that none of the dishes has pork, dairy or animal products, which can reflect an allergy or a religious belief. That is why the permission below is explicit.

Your permission comes first, on its own screen. The first time you reach for one of the three features, the app asks one question, "Allow AI features?", on a screen of its own. It comes before the Premium offer screen and before any sign-in, so the permission is never part of a purchase. It says what each feature sends, that it goes through our server to Google's Gemini, and that the list of dishes can reflect what your household avoids. You choose "Allow" or "Not now".

  • "Not now" sends nothing and opens neither the Premium offer screen nor a sign-in. The rest of the app keeps working: menus stay within one cuisine, each dish keeps its own recipe, and a dish you typed stays on your calendar under its name.
  • One permission covers all three features. The app keeps your answer on your phone, with its date and the version of the question's words. If the words change in what they say, the app asks again the next time you reach for a feature.
  • You can withdraw it at any time in Settings → Privacy & security → AI features. The three features then stop until you allow them again.
  • A Premium offer screen you reach any other way asks nothing about AI; it says that some Premium features use Google's Gemini and ask your permission before you first use them.

The permission is never asked when the app starts or while you set it up. Before anything is sent, the app checks that you have Premium, and our server checks it too, with a short-lived ticket that names no account and expires within an hour.

What we keep: one thing, the dish name. Our server keeps nothing of the requests or the answers, with one exception. When you ask for a recipe for a dish our catalogue doesn't have, we count the name, so that if many people ask for the same dish we know to add it properly. We store the name in a simplified form, how it was most recently spelled, a running count, the languages it was asked in, and the date and time it was first and last asked for. Nothing else: no account, no device, no address. For a name only one person has ever asked for, those times are when that person asked. These counts are kept with no end date. The recipe written for you stays on your phone for a week, so it doesn't change while you are cooking it, and reaches our server only if you report a problem with it.

What Google does with these requests. We use Gemini as a paid service. Under Google's terms for it, Google doesn't use what we send or what it returns to improve its products or to train its models, and it processes them on our behalf under its data processing terms. Google keeps requests and answers for up to 55 days solely to detect and prevent abuse of its service, and during that time authorized Google staff may review them for that purpose. We cannot match those records to you, so we cannot find or delete them on request; they expire on their own.

A failure never costs you a recipe: the app falls back to a menu built on the phone, to each dish's own recipe, or to the dish on your calendar under the name you typed. We make no decision about you with a legal or similar effect by automated means.

RevenueCat checks it for us. RevenueCat, Inc. is our service provider for subscriptions. Once you are signed in it asks RevenueCat which plan you are on, whether or not you have ever bought anything, under your account's ID and no other ID; so RevenueCat holds a record for every signed-in account. The app has switched off the library's collection of advertising and attribution identifiers. On iPhone the library still sends Apple's identifier for this app on this phone with each request. The subscription emails we send.   • A yearly reminder for every other subscription, once a year on the anniversary of the first purchase.

Emails to subscribers in Canada are in French first, then English. They are factual and carry no offer and no unsubscribe link, because each is a notice the law requires or a message about your subscription. A cancellation sends no email; it cancels the notices still waiting.

What we store to send them. No email address is stored for this. When an email is due, our server reads your address from the sign-in service for that one send and passes it to Resend. We keep, linked to your account's ID, a record of the subscription (the store, product, plan period, trial status, country and currency, price, key dates and whether it renews) and a row for each notice (its kind, when it is due, whether it was sent). Premium never changes how your safety settings work. Allergen filtering, lactose avoidance and the vegetarian, vegan, pescatarian, halal and kosher filters work without Premium and without an account, and nothing about them is sent to RevenueCat.

Cancelling, and deleting your account. Usage statistics

The app can count how it is used so that we know how many people use it each month and can decide what to improve. A library from PostHog, Inc. does the counting in the app and sends the counts to PostHog's EU cloud in Frankfurt, Germany.

Off until you say yes — everywhere. Nothing is counted and nothing is sent, and the library is not started, until you choose "Allow". The app asks once, after you finish setting up, with the choices "Allow" and "No thanks". Saying no costs you nothing: no feature, and not Premium, depends on your answer. You can change your mind at any time in Settings → Privacy & security → Usage statistics.

What is counted, and what never is. Each count is an event with a name and a few values chosen from fixed lists in the app, such as: the app was opened; a screen was viewed; suggestions were requested (the kind of request, how many came back, the cuisine asked for); a menu was opened; a dish was marked as cooked; the calendar, shopping list or pantry was used; a home-screen widget was added; something was shared; a report was sent; an AI feature was used and whether it answered; the Premium screen was shown; one of the occasional Premium suggestions was shown or closed; and a purchase was started, completed or cancelled. Every event also carries the app's version, the platform, the app's language and region, your residence country (only United States or Canada) and whether you have Premium. Never: anything about your health or diet — your allergies, intolerances, diets or disliked ingredients, or any filter built from them (the cuisine you asked for and the kind of request are counted); the people you cook for; a dish name or any other text you typed; which recipe, pantry item or shopping item; notes and ratings; your account ID or email address. The app never tells PostHog who you are, and no person profile is created.

An ID for the phone, not for you. PostHog's library creates a random ID on your phone and a device ID derived from it. Neither is linked to your account. A random ID is not anonymous: it lets the counts from one phone be recognised as coming from the same phone, and this notice does not call the statistics anonymous. The library also adds technical details about the phone to each event, such as its model, operating system and time zone. PostHog receives your phone's IP address with each request, as every server does, but our project is set to discard it, and each event also asks PostHog not to work out a location from it.

Turning them off deletes everything the library kept on your phone, including its IDs; if you switch them on again, it starts with new IDs, so what was sent before cannot be joined to what is sent after. Nothing in the statistics is used for advertising, sold or given to anybody else.

Reports about a recipe

Only when you send one. At the foot of every recipe — ours, and the ones the app writes for a dish you typed — "Report a problem with this recipe" lets you tell us what is wrong. You pick a reason and can add a note of up to 500 characters. Nothing is sent until you tap "Send report", and a report that could not be sent is not saved or sent later on its own. Reporting does not need Premium, an account or the permission for the AI features.

What is sent and kept. For a recipe from our catalogue: its catalogue code, the reason, your note and the app's language — not the recipe, which we already have. For a recipe the app wrote for a dish you typed, its title, ingredients and steps go with the report, because that recipe exists only on your phone; the report screen says so before you send. Never your account, your household, your allergies or your profile. We keep the report, with the day it was sent (not the time of day), for 12 months, then delete it. Nothing about who sent it is stored with it: no account, no device ID, no IP address, and nothing is sent back to your phone that could find it again. To stop abuse, our server refuses more than ten reports a day from one internet address or home network; it counts them under a keyed code that changes every day, which we do not turn back into the address and never store with a report, and deletes each day's counts shortly after the day ends.

Please keep personal details out of the note. We cannot reply to a report, and a name or an email address written into the note would be kept for the 12 months like the rest. If you want an answer, write to support@pinjula.com. To have a report you sent deleted sooner, write to us with enough to find it — the recipe, the day and the words of your note — and we will delete it. We read reports to correct the catalogue and the way the app writes recipes; a report is not a promise that anything will change.

  • Reports about a recipe: 12 months from the day they were sent, then deleted.
  • Usage statistics: on your phone, until you turn statistics off. At PostHog: one year from the event, then deleted.

Deleting your account

If you never signed in, we hold no account for you; uninstalling the app deletes everything it stores on your phone.

In the app. Settings → Account → Delete account, or Settings → Privacy & security → Delete account. The app tells you that billing for a Premium subscription continues through the store until you cancel it there and that our subscription emails stop, and links to where you can cancel; it then shows what will be deleted and asks you to confirm twice, the second time by typing a word. The page pinjula.com/delete-account/ describes the same steps, and the same request can be made by email to support@pinjula.com.

What is deleted.

  • At once, on your phone: the app's data — everyone's declarations, your ratings, your widgets and reminders, the usage-statistics IDs, the downloaded dish pictures and the copies the photo picker left in the app's cache. Sign in with Apple. Your account is deleted all the same when the revocation does not go through — for example if you cancel Apple's prompt. Children and age

You must be 18 or older to use Pinjula, and the app is not directed at children. That entry, with anything recorded for the child, stays on the adult's phone and is never sent to us. We hold nothing about children, and we do not knowingly collect personal information from anyone under 18.

Ingredients you avoid, and sensitive information

For each person you cook for, you can record what they avoid: allergies (with how serious), intolerances such as lactose, diets (halal and kosher among them) and ingredients they dislike. We don't ask why. Some entries may reflect an allergy, a health condition or a belief, so we treat the whole list as sensitive: it stays on your phone and we never receive it. It is never sent to Google, to RevenueCat or with the usage statistics, and reminders and widgets never show it.

One thing can still reflect it, and it needs your explicit consent. The catalogue dishes sent for a menu — to mix cuisines, or to plan how to cook it — were chosen on your phone with your household's filters, so the list can reflect an allergy or a religious belief. The AI-features question says so in plain words before you agree, and you can withdraw that consent at any time (see "The AI features and your permission"). We never use sensitive information for advertising, to profile you, or to train any model, and we do not sell it.

Once on first entry. The first time you enter a person's details, the app tells you in one note that they stay on your phone and are never sent to us, and that your phone's own backup to your Google or Apple account may include them.

Not medical or nutritional advice

Where the app shows nutrition figures, they are estimates calculated from published food-composition databases (USDA FoodData Central and, for some ingredients, the Australian Food Composition Database by Food Standards Australia New Zealand) applied to a recipe's ingredients — not an analysis of the food you cook, and never medical or dietary advice. Allergen filtering is a best-effort aid, not a guarantee: recipes containing your listed allergens are filtered out, which does not make any recipe safe or free from an allergen. Always check the full ingredient list and the label of every product yourself before cooking, especially if anyone at the table has a food allergy. A recipe the app wrote for a dish you typed carries no allergen, diet or nutrition information at all. The app is not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any medical condition; for medical advice, consult a healthcare professional. In an emergency, call 911 or your local emergency number.
